Opinion: Antisemitism cannot be redefined out of existence

Students are free to harshly criticize the Israeli government. Many progressive Jews do exactly this and still face antisemitism.
  • Maxwell Fine
  • September 10, 2026

For Jewish students, the Government of Canada’s Campus Antisemitism and Student Experiences report (CASE) does not describe an abstract problem. Among voluntary Jewish respondents, 95.7 percent—so, the vast majority—”said they had experienced or witnessed antisemitism” in the past year, while 81 percent mentioned “hateful or discriminatory treatment of Zionists on campus.” Campus antisemitism cannot be redefined out of existence. These numbers demand a question: what are we going to do to change the culture that produces this hostility?

I am an astrophysics Ph.D. student at McGill University. I studied at the University of Toronto (UofT) from 2019 to 2023, where I was elected to the student union and a Hillel UofT executive. I experienced much of the antisemitism described in the report and spent years fighting against it. I challenged student-union policies that discriminated against Jewish students, including blacklisting Israeli-Palestinian organizations such as Standing Together and cancelling antisemitism training by a Black-Jewish EDI organization the union had hired, before banning it. 

A class-action lawsuit against McGill alleging that the university failed to protect Jewish students from antisemitism has recently been authorized to proceed. Universities have responsibilities, and when they fail to meet them, there must be consequences. 

However, a lawsuit cannot change the campus culture that produces antisemitism. It cannot make students who believe that Jewish students are inherently opposed to Palestinians challenge that belief, or confront the antisemitic conspiracies that they have absorbed. Nor can a lawsuit make Jewish students safer when campus culture fails them—the hostility faced by Jewish survivors of sexual assault, or that faced by LGBTQ+ Jewish students. 

Legal accountability is insufficient. We need a proactive strategy to transform campus. Changing campus culture is much harder than winning a lawsuit, and it too requires sustained effort over years. The on-campus Jewish community—Hillel, the Canadian Union of Jewish Students (CUJS), AEPI, and Chabad do valuable work. However, it should not be the job of Jewish students to prove that we have a right to belong, and that our culture is valid. We must have a campus where Jewish students—like all students—belong.

Universities do not need to make students agree about the Middle East. That is not their job. Their job is considerably less ambitious—to ensure that Jewish students are not treated as a foreign government. It is absurd that I have to write this: students are free to harshly criticize the Israeli government, protest Israeli policies, advocate for Palestine, and the Palestinian people. Many progressive Jews like myself do exactly this and still face antisemitism. Being pro-Palestine is not the issue. Racism does not become justified when dressed in the language of social justice.

Israeli research shows that meaningful intergroup contact improves attitudes and relationships when institutions create sustained interactions. An imperfect example: Brown University. During the 2024 campus protests, 24% of Brown’s students were Jewish—a community too large for the student body to erase. Pro-Palestine student protestors themselves pushed back against slogans they viewed as antisemitic or supportive of violence, including “Globalize the Intifada”. 

Bringing people like Aziz Abu-Sarah, Dara Horn, and Hen Mazzig to campus is valuable, but one-off events are not enough. In Canada, Peace Now UofT and York’s Bridging the Gap have tried to create this type of change. However, this work should not fall on the students willing to do it themselves. Universities have a legal obligation to ensure that Jewish students are not subjected to discrimination and hostile campus environments. Universities must invest in recurring intergroup contact. It is less costly than lawsuits. This means shared meals, cultural events, small-group discussions, collaborative projects, exchanges, and conversations with Israeli & Palestinian peace activists. Student-union executives and influential campus leaders, including professors, should be obligated to attend. The goal is not dialogue, it is making the absurdity of antisemitism impossible to ignore.

Efforts will fail. No strategy will eliminate antisemitism entirely. That is not evidence that the strategy is pointless; it is evidence that fighting antisemitism is difficult. The alternative cannot remain filing complaints that go ignored or launching another lawsuit. 

The recommendations from the CASE team are not enough. Enforcement of the same failed EDI policies is not enough. 

The solution cannot be limited to improved reporting, and punishing antisemitism retroactively. It has to be making antisemitism socially unthinkable: to create a campus where treating Jewish students as an extension of the Israeli government is obviously wrong. To borrow a phrase from a Columbia University Jewish students’ statement, “We are your lab partners, your study buddies, your peers and your friends.” Our response should be defiant: we will not withdraw from campus because others have decided that Jews do not belong.

Maxwell Fine is an astrophysics Ph.D. student at McGill University, where he studies fast radio bursts—bright flashes of radio light from distant galaxies. He previously studied at the University of Amsterdam and the University of Toronto (UofT). As an undergraduate, he held leadership positions in the Student Union and Jewish student organisations at UofT, including Peace Now UofT, and Hillel UofT. He was an invited panelist at the ADL/Hillel International Conference on fighting antisemitism in 2022. He can be found on Instagram @afinemax. The views he expresses here are his own.
